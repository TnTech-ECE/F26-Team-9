## Function of the Subsystem

This segment should elucidate the role of the subsystem within the entire system, detailing its intended function, aligned with the conceptual design.




The Microcontroller and Control Logic subsystem serves as the primary decision-making component of the proposed variable-speed circulation pump controller. This subsystem is responsible for receiving operating measurements from the Sensor and Data Acquisition subsystem, processing those measurements using a programmed control algorithm, and determining the appropriate circulation pump speed for the current operating conditions of the water heating system.

The overall objective of the project is to investigate whether adjusting the circulation pump speed can reduce unnecessary electrical energy consumption without compromising the performance of Lochinvar's Regent Commercial Instantaneous Water Heater. Because water demand and heating requirements can change throughout the day, operating the circulation pump at a constant speed may result in more electrical energy being consumed than necessary. The proposed controller will address this by evaluating multiple operating parameters and adjusting the pump-speed command when conditions allow.

As established in the conceptual design, the team selected a control strategy that combines water flow rate, boiler firing rate, and outlet-temperature feedback. The water flow rate and boiler firing rate will provide information about the current operating conditions and will be used to determine an initial pump-speed command. The outlet-water temperature will then provide feedback that allows the controller to evaluate whether the current pump operation is maintaining the required temperature performance. This combination was selected because it considers multiple aspects of system operation rather than depending entirely on a single measurement.

The microcontroller will receive the necessary measurements from the Sensor and Data Acquisition subsystem. These measurements may include the water flow rate in gallons per minute, inlet and outlet water temperatures in degrees Fahrenheit, and boiler firing rate as a percentage of the maximum firing capacity. The controller will use the available information to determine the pump-speed command, which will be transmitted to the Pump and Motor Control subsystem through a compatible electrical interface.

After the pump speed is adjusted, the system will continue collecting measurements to determine how the change affects water flow, temperature stability, and overall heating performance. The controller will repeat this process during operation, allowing the pump-speed command to respond to changing demand.

An important responsibility of this subsystem is ensuring that pump-speed adjustments remain within the operating limitations of the selected equipment. Although reducing pump speed may decrease electrical power consumption, reducing circulation below the manufacturer's minimum flow requirement could cause the water heater to operate outside its approved conditions. Therefore, the controller must evaluate the measured flow rate and prevent energy-saving adjustments from taking priority over the minimum circulation requirements.

The subsystem must also operate without replacing or interfering with the existing Lochinvar temperature regulation and safety controls. The proposed controller is intended to supplement the existing system by improving circulation pump operation, not take over the boiler's internal heating or safety functions.

The Microcontroller and Control Logic subsystem will also provide operating information to the Data Logging and Simulation subsystem. This information will allow the team to record the controller's decisions, compare pump-speed commands with measured system responses, and evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm.

Ultimately, this subsystem will connect the physical measurements obtained from the water heating system to the control decisions responsible for adjusting pump speed. Its success will depend on its ability to process measurements accurately, respond to changes in demand, maintain the required operating limits, and support measurable reductions in pump electrical power consumption.

### Electrical Specifications
---

The electrical design must be compatible with the selected microcontroller, sensors, boiler interface, and pump-control hardware.

The conceptual design identifies the boiler firing-rate signal as a 0–10 V analog signal representing 0–100% firing rate. This voltage is higher than the allowable analog input range of many commonly used microcontrollers. Therefore, the Sensor and Data Acquisition subsystem must provide appropriate signal conditioning before the measurement reaches the microcontroller.

For a proposed microcontroller operating with a 3.3 V analog reference, the firing-rate input may be scaled to a lower voltage using a properly designed analog conditioning circuit. The exact circuit will depend on the selected microcontroller, input impedance requirements, grounding arrangement, and electrical protection requirements.

The flow sensor identified in the project documentation is the KEYENCE FD-Q50C. The project intends to use pulse-frequency information to determine water flow rate. Before implementing the interface, the team must confirm the actual sensor output configuration and electrical characteristics from its datasheet.

If a compatible pulse output is available, the microcontroller may determine the flow rate by measuring the number of pulses received over a known time interval or by measuring the period between consecutive pulses.

The controller output will depend on the selected pump and motor-control equipment. A pump controller may accept an analog speed reference, a pulse-width-modulated signal, or a digital communication command. The microcontroller output circuitry must be designed around the actual requirements of the selected equipment.


### Operating Constraints
---

The controller must operate within the physical limitations of the water heating system. These limitations include the minimum required circulation flow, maximum allowable pump speed, permitted electrical signal ranges, and acceptable outlet-temperature variation.

The project has established a steady-state temperature stability target of ±4°F relative to the desired outlet-temperature setpoint. The controller must support this requirement while allowing pump speed to decrease when operating conditions permit.

However, temperature performance alone cannot determine whether a pump-speed command is acceptable. A command that produces an acceptable outlet temperature but reduces flow below the manufacturer's minimum requirement must still be considered unacceptable.

For this reason, minimum-flow protection will take priority over energy-saving calculations.

The controller must also consider the relationship between pump speed and water temperature. Increasing circulation flow does not necessarily increase outlet temperature under every operating condition. The effect depends on heat transfer, boiler firing rate, inlet temperature, and system configuration. The controller's response direction must therefore be established through simulation and experimental testing rather than assumed.


### Safety, Ethical, and Standards Constraints
---

The subsystem must be designed with electrical safety, equipment protection, and public welfare in mind.

The project documentation identifies NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code, as a reference for applicable electrical installation requirements. Any permanent electrical connections must be reviewed for appropriate wiring, grounding, protection, and installation practices.

The project also identifies OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147 as a reference for hazardous-energy control during servicing and maintenance where applicable. Laboratory procedures must account for the risks associated with energized equipment, hot water, and circulating machinery.

From an ethical standpoint, the controller must prioritize safe and reliable operation over potential energy savings. If a sensor fails or the controller receives invalid operating data, it must not continue making unrestricted pump-speed reductions.

The exact response to a fault will depend on the existing Lochinvar system and the selected pump controller. A safe response may involve transferring control to an approved fallback mode, maintaining an approved circulation condition, or requesting a controlled shutdown through an authorized interface. The team must establish this response with Lochinvar before implementation.


## Overview of Proposed Solution

Describe the solution and how it will fulfill the specifications and constraints of this subsystem.



The proposed Microcontroller and Control Logic subsystem will use an embedded microcontroller to execute a control algorithm that determines circulation pump speed from measured operating conditions. The implementation will consist of measurement processing, operating-state evaluation, pump-speed calculation, output limiting, fault monitoring, and communication with the other subsystems. The proposed control algorithm will operate in a repeating sequence. First, the microcontroller will receive the most recent flow-rate, firing-rate, and temperature measurements. The controller will check these measurements to determine whether they are valid and within their expected ranges.

Once the measurements have been validated, the controller will calculate an initial pump-speed command based on water flow rate and boiler firing rate. This initial command will represent the pump operation expected to be appropriate for the current heating demand. The controller will then evaluate outlet-temperature feedback. The measured outlet temperature will be compared with the desired temperature setpoint to calculate the temperature error.

The temperature error can be expressed as:
\[
e_T(t)=T_{\text{setpoint}}-T_{\text{outlet}}(t)
\]

where \(e_T(t)\) is the temperature error, \(T_{\text{setpoint}}\) is the desired outlet-water temperature, and \(T_{\text{outlet}}(t)\) is the measured outlet-water temperature.

The controller will use this error to determine whether the initial pump-speed command should be adjusted. The amount and direction of the adjustment will depend on the experimentally established relationship between pump speed and outlet temperature.

A proposed control structure is:
\[
u_{\text{requested}}(t)=u_{\text{initial}}(t)+u_{\text{feedback}}(t)
\]

where \(u_{\text{initial}}\) is the initial demand-based command and \(u_{\text{feedback}}\) is the correction calculated from temperature feedback.

Before the command is transmitted to the pump, the controller will apply operating limits.
\[
u_{\text{limited}}=
\operatorname{clamp}
\left(u_{\text{requested}},u_{\min},u_{\max}\right)
\]

where \(u_{\min}\) and \(u_{\max}\) represent the permitted command limits.

These command limits must be determined from the selected pump specifications and validated flow measurements. A fixed minimum-speed command alone cannot guarantee minimum flow under all hydraulic conditions, so the measured flow rate must remain part of the protective logic.

### Microcontroller Selection
---

The microcontroller must provide sufficient processing capability, memory, electrical interfaces, and timing resources to execute the control algorithm reliably. The selected microcontroller should support analog-to-digital conversion, digital input capture or pulse counting, communication with external equipment, and periodic execution of the control algorithm. A platform such as the STM32L476RG could be evaluated because it provides these general capabilities. However, the team has not finalized the microcontroller selection in the supplied midterm design document. The final selection will depend on the number and type of sensor inputs, the pump-control interface, the required communication method, and the available project budget.

### Software Architecture 
--- 

* The controller software will be divided into several functional sections to make development, testing, and troubleshooting more manageable.

* The initialization section will configure the microcontroller peripherals, establish operating parameters, and verify that the required hardware interfaces are available.

* The measurement-processing section will receive sensor values and convert them into engineering units. It will also identify measurements that are missing, invalid, or outside the expected range.

* The control-calculation section will use the validated measurements to determine the appropriate pump-speed command.

* The safety-monitoring section will evaluate minimum-flow conditions, pump operating limits, and detected faults.

* The output section will transmit the final speed command to the Pump and Motor Control subsystem.

* The communication and logging section will provide the measurements, controller status, and calculated commands needed by the Data Logging and Simulation subsystem.

* Separating these functions will allow the team to test individual portions of the software before evaluating the complete controller.

### Controller Operating States
--- 

A state-based software structure is proposed to organize the controller's operation.

| Operating state | Intended behavior |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Initialization | Configure hardware and verify operating parameters |
| Standby | Monitor the system while waiting for an authorized operating condition |
| Normal operation | Calculate and transmit pump-speed commands |
| Temperature recovery | Apply the validated response when temperature performance requires correction |
| Minimum-flow protection | Prioritize circulation requirements over energy savings |
| Fault condition | Execute the approved response to invalid data or unsafe conditions |


The transitions between these states will be based on sensor measurements, operating limits, and fault conditions.

The final transition thresholds must be established before the software is approved for physical implementation.


## Interface with Other Subsystems

Provide detailed information about the inputs, outputs, and data transferred to other subsystems. Ensure specificity and thoroughness, clarifying the method of communication and the nature of the data transmitted.



The Microcontroller and Control Logic subsystem must exchange information with several other subsystems. These interfaces are necessary because the microcontroller does not directly measure the physical operating conditions or supply motor power. Instead, it receives measurements from the sensing equipment, processes the information, and sends control instructions to the pump hardware.


### Sensor and Data Acquisition Interface
---

The Sensor and Data Acquisition subsystem provides the measurements required for the control algorithm. These measurements include water flow rate, boiler firing rate, and available temperature measurements.

The flow sensor will provide information about water movement through the circulation system. The boiler firing-rate signal will provide an indication of the current heating output, while the temperature measurements will allow the controller to evaluate thermal performance.

The sensor subsystem will be responsible for the electrical conditioning necessary to make these measurements compatible with the microcontroller.

For the proposed design, the microcontroller will use the received measurements to calculate operating values in engineering units. The controller will also verify that the values remain within the expected operating ranges before using them in control calculations.


### Pump and Motor Control Interface
---

The Pump and Motor Control subsystem will receive the pump-speed command generated by the microcontroller.

The command will represent the desired operating speed, either as a percentage of the permitted range or in another format supported by the selected pump-control equipment.

For example, if the selected pump controller accepts a 0–10 V analog speed reference, the microcontroller would require a compatible output circuit capable of producing that signal. The output circuit may include a digital-to-analog converter, signal-conditioning stage, and electrical protection.

However, the team must verify whether the selected pump supports this type of interface before designing the final circuit.

The Pump and Motor Control subsystem will be responsible for converting the command into the electrical operation required by the motor.

### Data Logging and Simulation Interface
---

The Microcontroller and Control Logic subsystem will provide operating measurements and control information to the Data Logging and Simulation subsystem.

This information will be used to evaluate how the controller responds to changes in water demand and whether the pump-speed adjustments produce the expected results.

The transferred information should include the measured flow rate, inlet and outlet temperatures, boiler firing rate, calculated pump-speed command, controller operating state, and detected fault conditions.

A proposed implementation is to transmit this information through a serial communication interface to a computer used for data collection.

The exact protocol and communication speed will be determined after the microcontroller and data-logging hardware have been selected.

This diagram is intentionally labeled as a reference architecture rather than a final buildable schematic because the exact field sensor output and pump command requirements are not yet confirmed. It is appropriate for explaining how the subsystem will be organized, but it must be replaced or supplemented with a component-level schematic before construction approval.

A preliminary 0–10 V measurement example uses a divider with R1 = 22 kΩ from the field signal to the ADC node and R2 = 10 kΩ from that node to the signal reference. For an ideal 10 V input, the divider produces V_ADC = 10 × 10/(22 + 10) = 3.125 V, which is below a nominal 3.3 V ADC full-scale value. This example does not address all fault cases: a higher-than-expected input, ground-potential differences, surge events, or resistor failure could still damage the controller. A finalized design requires a reviewed protection strategy, resistor tolerances and power ratings, filtering, and confirmation that the signal source permits the divider's input loading. The field reference must not be tied to the microcontroller ground without evaluating the equipment grounding requirements.

For temperature acquisition, two thermocouple-interface modules based on a device such as the MAX31856 are possible reference components. Each sensor would connect to the appropriate thermocouple input terminals, while the digital interface would connect to a supported microcontroller SPI peripheral with separate chip-select signals. The selected thermocouple type, module supply voltage, logic compatibility, filtering, and connector polarity must be confirmed. The flow input requires its own final circuit after the Keyence model and output mode are known. A 24 V industrial sensor output must not be connected directly to a 3.3 V logic pin. The pump-speed output must likewise use a compatible, appropriately isolated commercial interface or a fully engineered circuit that meets the drive's command specification. No mains-voltage pump wiring is proposed on the microcontroller board.

## Flowchart

For sections including a software component, produce a chart that demonstrates the decision-making process of the microcontroller. It should provide an overview of the device's function without exhaustive detail.


The controller flowchart, subsystem2_flowchart.svg, describes the proposed decision making sequence. After initialization, the controller acquires flow, firing-rate, and temperature measurements and checks their validity. An invalid required input causes the program to enter an approved fault state and record the reason. Valid measurements are used to calculate an initial pump-speed request and a temperature-feedback correction. The controller then applies command limits and checks measured flow against the required minimum before issuing a command.

The minimum-flow decision is a separate branch because it represents an operating constraint rather than an ordinary temperature correction. If flow is insufficient, the program must execute a protective response that has been approved for the actual equipment. The controller records the resulting state and continues monitoring for recovery conditions. The logging computer does not make this safety decision. The diagram uses decision diamonds for input validity and flow protection so that the operating sequence is clear without reproducing every software statement.

## Analysis

Deliver a full and relevant analysis of the design demonstrating that it should meet the constraints and accomplish the intended function. This analysis should be comprehensive and well articulated for persuasiveness.



The conceptual design requires the controller to process available flow, temperature, and boiler measurements; calculate an operating-point-dependent speed request; keep commands within established limits; provide relevant operating data; and respond to missing or implausible measurements. These requirements must be tested individually before integrated testing. The intended Regent Commercial Instantaneous Water Heater remains the design target, but the project proposal explicitly permits compatible Lochinvar laboratory equipment or simulation if access is limited. Therefore, an acceptance result obtained on a different test rig must identify the equipment and cannot automatically be generalized to the Regent without further verification.

The ±4°F temperature target, manufacturer minimum flow, time-to-setpoint, and electrical energy use are coupled constraints. For example, a lower pump command may reduce motor electrical power but may also alter circulation flow and heat transfer. A valid controller decision must be evaluated against all relevant constraints rather than against pump speed alone. The controller must also retain the boiler's own protections; the external algorithm is not a substitute for equipment safety interlocks.

The engineering rationale for the proposed controller is that pump electrical consumption may be reduced when circulation speed can be lowered without violating heating or hydraulic requirements. For a centrifugal pump operating under conditions where affinity-law approximations are appropriate, power is sometimes represented as varying approximately with the cube of rotational speed. The illustrative relationship is P₂/P₁ ≈ (N₂/N₁)³. At 80% of the reference speed, the idealized power ratio is 0.8³ = 0.512, corresponding to a theoretical reduction of 48.8%. This value is not a prediction for the Lochinvar system. Actual electrical power depends on the pump curve, motor efficiency, drive losses, system resistance, and operating point, so final conclusions must be based on measured electrical power.

Temperature performance introduces a competing objective. Lowering flow may increase the temperature rise across a heat exchanger under some conditions, but the boiler's internal control response and system dynamics may change the overall result. The controller therefore cannot rely on a fixed assumption that increasing or decreasing speed always moves the outlet temperature in a particular direction. The relationship should be characterized experimentally and represented in the simulation before feedback gains or lookup-table corrections are finalized. A rate limit and an appropriately chosen control interval may reduce unnecessary command fluctuations, while the minimum-flow constraint prevents the efficiency objective from overriding equipment protection.

The firing-rate measurement also requires an electrical accuracy analysis. With the example 22 kΩ/10 kΩ divider, the nominal scaling factor is 0.3125, and the original signal can be reconstructed as V_firing = 3.2 × V_ADC. The accuracy of that reconstruction depends on resistor tolerance, ADC calibration, reference voltage, source impedance, and noise. Thermocouple accuracy must similarly account for sensor installation, interface error, and cold-junction compensation. These measurement uncertainties matter because the controller is expected to regulate a relatively narrow temperature band. A final design analysis should calculate the combined temperature measurement uncertainty and confirm that the selected hardware is suitable for evaluating the ±4°F target.

Verification will proceed from low-risk interface tests to integrated operation. The firing-rate input can be evaluated using a controlled low-voltage source, and the thermocouple interfaces can be checked against a reference temperature measurement. The flow input should be validated against the selected sensor's calibration and a suitable reference. Pump-command outputs should initially be tested with a simulated or disconnected load that represents the intended command interface, not with unreviewed wiring to an operating boiler. Fault tests should include invalid sensor values, missing records, and controller restart. Integrated tests will then compare baseline and variable-speed operation under comparable thermal conditions, recording minimum flow, outlet-temperature deviation, time-to-setpoint, and measured pump electrical energy.

The principal limitations are the unconfirmed boiler model, flow threshold, pump command method, sensor electrical outputs, and control coefficients. These are not minor documentation omissions; they determine whether the final circuit and controller behavior are safe and technically correct. The reference implementation establishes a defensible architecture, but completion requires component selection, sponsor review, a buildable schematic, and experimental validation.
