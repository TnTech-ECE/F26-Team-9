## Function of the Subsystem

This segment should elucidate the role of the subsystem within the entire system, detailing its intended function, aligned with the conceptual design.



The Microcontroller and Control Logic subsystem is identified as Atomic Subsystem 2 in the team conceptual design and forms the decision-making portion of the proposed Lochinvar Regent Commercial Instantaneous Water Heater variable-speed circulation pump controller. Its primary responsibility is to transform measurements of water flow, boiler firing demand, and water temperature into an appropriate pump-speed request. The proposed strategy is intended to reduce avoidable circulation-pump electricity consumption during periods of reduced heating demand while maintaining the temperature performance and hydraulic operating limits of the water heating equipment. Unlike a monitoring device that only displays measurements, this subsystem must continuously evaluate system conditions and determine whether a speed adjustment is appropriate. Its output directly affects circulation flow, so the controller must place equipment protection and operating stability ahead of an attempted energy reduction.

The controller will use flow rate and boiler firing rate as indications of the current operating condition. A feedforward calculation will establish an initial speed request, after which outlet-temperature feedback will provide a correction based on the difference between the measured temperature and the selected setpoint. The controller will then enforce allowable pump commands and verify that measured circulation flow satisfies the applicable minimum requirement. This sequence makes the control decision dependent on both heating demand and the actual hydraulic response. The controller is not intended to replace the boiler's combustion management, over-temperature protection, or manufacturer-installed safety functions. Instead, it is a supplementary pump-speed control function whose authority must be limited to an approved interface.

The project conceptual design divides the overall solution into six interacting subsystems: Sensor and Data Acquisition, Microcontroller and Control Logic, Pump and Motor Control, Water Heating and Hydraulic, Monitoring and Performance Evaluation, and Data Logging and Simulation. The controller receives conditioned measurements from Sensor and Data Acquisition, transmits an approved speed request to Pump and Motor Control, and is influenced by the physical behavior of Water Heating and Hydraulic. The Monitoring and Performance Evaluation subsystem independently assesses the results. The subsystem also provides operating measurements and status information to the Data Logging and Simulation subsystem. Recording the requested speed, flow rate, firing rate, inlet and outlet temperatures, and fault state allows the project to evaluate the relationship between control actions and measured performance. This connection is important because a reduced pump-speed command does not, by itself, demonstrate an electrical energy saving or acceptable heating performance. The physical implementation must therefore support reliable measurement, deterministic control behavior, and traceable data transmission.



## Specifications and Constraints

This section should provide a list of constraints applicable to the subsystem, along with the rationale behind these limitations. For instance, constraints can stem from physics-based limitations or requirements, subsystem prerequisites, standards, ethical considerations, or socio-economic factors.

The team should set specifications for each subsystem. These specifications may require modifications, which must be authorized by the team. It could be necessary to impose additional constraints as further information becomes available.

Every subsystem must incorporate at least one constraint stemming from standards, ethics, or socio-economic factors.



The central thermal specification is to maintain the outlet water temperature within approximately ±4°F of the selected setpoint during a defined steady-state evaluation period. This requirement must be interpreted carefully: the boiler must have sufficient heating capacity to satisfy the demand, the temperature sensor must be properly installed, and the acceptance test must distinguish startup or demand transients from steady-state operation. Temperature error is defined as e(t) = T_setpoint − T_outlet(t). The magnitude and duration of this error will be recorded so that the control method can be evaluated against the project target. The control design must also avoid a significant increase in time-to-setpoint compared with the baseline operating method. A numerical limit for that increase must be agreed upon before acceptance testing rather than chosen after results are available.

The most important hydraulic constraint is the manufacturer-required minimum water flow through the selected heating equipment. The actual minimum in gallons per minute is not yet established because the specific equipment model and applicable operating conditions must be verified. The controller must therefore treat this value as a documented configuration parameter rather than an assumed constant. A speed command may satisfy the pump's electrical command range and still produce insufficient water flow if system resistance changes. For that reason, a minimum-speed setting alone cannot serve as the complete flow-protection mechanism. A measured-flow check and an approved response to insufficient flow must be included in the operating logic.

The electrical interfaces introduce additional design constraints. The expected boiler firing-rate signal is 0–10 V, while many microcontroller analog inputs are limited to approximately 3.3 V. An appropriate divider or conditioning module must reduce the input voltage, and additional protection must be evaluated for transients, wiring faults, and electrical noise. Thermocouple sensors generate small voltages and require compatible interfaces with cold-junction compensation. The proposed KEYENCE FD-Q50C flow sensor is described in the project proposal as providing a pulse-frequency output, but the actual configured electrical output and voltage levels must be checked against its datasheet; therefore, its output cannot be connected directly to a microcontroller pin until its electrical characteristics have been confirmed. The pump output similarly depends on the selected drive's supported command interface and its required fail-safe behavior.

Reliability is a functional and ethical constraint because the controller operates near electrical equipment and heated water. Invalid sensor readings, a disconnected signal, a frozen control task, loss of communication, and controller power interruption must produce defined responses that are consistent with manufacturer requirements. Applicable electrical installation requirements, including relevant provisions of NFPA 70, must be considered for the actual installation. Any work on energized equipment must follow the laboratory's approved procedures. The design must not bypass the boiler's existing protective devices. Economic constraints also favor a controller that uses available development tools and commercially supported sensor interfaces without adding components that provide no measurable improvement in reliability or performance.

### Proposed Implementation Traceability
--- 

The project proposal identifies the KEYENCE FD-Q50C clamp-on flow sensor, two Cooper-Atkins 4005MK Type-K pipe-clamp temperature sensors, and a variable-speed circulation pump as the planned primary components. These are proposed purchasing selections rather than evidence of installed, calibrated hardware. The flow sensor is described in the proposal as supplying a pulse-frequency signal that will be converted to gallons per minute. Before implementing the microcontroller timer input, the team must verify the selected sensor's actual pulse configuration, supply voltage, output stage, and frequency-to-flow relationship. Similarly, the Type-K sensors require a compatible thermocouple measurement interface and cold-junction compensation; they should not be treated as direct 0–3.3 V analog temperature sensors.

The team identified a 0–10 V firing-rate signal on the compatible Knight boiler, corresponding to 0–100% firing rate. The proposed controller will acquire that signal through an appropriately designed conditioning stage. This must be verified against the specific boiler's interface documentation, including the reference ground and allowable loading. The test equipment available at Tennessee Tech includes Lochinvar Knight KHB085 and WHB199 fire-tube boilers, while the Regent remains the intended application. A controller calibrated on a Knight installation cannot automatically be assumed to have identical dynamics or compatible connections on a Regent; those differences must be documented before transferring the results.

## Overview of Proposed Solution

Describe the solution and how it will fulfill the specifications and constraints of this subsystem.



The detailed design follows Solution Five selected in the team conceptual design after comparison with firing-rate-only control, flow-only control, temperature-only feedback, state-based control, and simulation-only investigation. The selected method combines water flow rate and boiler firing rate to calculate the initial pump-speed command, then evaluates outlet-temperature feedback before issuing the final request. This distinction is important: simulation is a supporting evaluation tool rather than the chosen real-time controller, and a single measurement is not sufficient to describe every operating condition. The design must remain compatible with the actual laboratory equipment, which may be a different Lochinvar unit from the intended Regent application.

The design will be developed as a bounded state machine rather than as an unrestricted continuous command. At startup, the program will verify sensor availability and approved operating configuration. During normal operation, it will compute the feedforward request, apply a temperature correction derived from a measured and validated plant response, and check the resulting command against the drive limits and minimum measured flow. If a critical measurement is missing or an operating limit is violated, the controller will execute a preapproved response instead of attempting to optimize electrical efficiency. The final response must be reviewed with Lochinvar because simply stopping the pump may be unsafe for some hot-water equipment configurations.

A microcontroller-based implementation is proposed because a single programmable device can acquire sensor measurements, execute a periodic control task, generate a pump request, and transmit data to a computer. An STM32 NUCLEO-L476RG development board is a reference candidate rather than a finalized capstone selection. Its ADC, timers, SPI interfaces, and serial communication resources illustrate how the required functions could be implemented, but final pin assignments depend on the selected breakout modules and the board's supported peripheral configuration. The implementation should separate hardware acquisition from engineering-unit conversion and control decisions so that each portion can be tested independently.

At the beginning of each control cycle, the software will read the required measurements and validate them. The flow measurement will be converted into gallons per minute using the exact sensor's documented scaling. The expected linear 0–10 V firing-rate signal may be converted to a percentage using F(%) = 100 × V_firing / 10, but the signal's actual interpretation must be confirmed with Lochinvar. Inlet and outlet thermocouples will be read through compatible thermocouple interface devices, and their status flags will be checked before the temperatures are accepted. Measurements that fall outside documented sensor limits or fail communication checks must not be silently substituted with plausible values.

The initial speed request is represented as u_initial = f(Q, F), where Q is water flow and F is firing-rate demand. The function f may be implemented as a calibrated lookup table or another experimentally justified relationship. The temperature-feedback correction is represented as u_requested = u_initial + Δu_T. The sign and magnitude of Δu_T must be based on the observed thermal response of the actual system; increasing pump speed does not always produce the same outlet-temperature effect under every heating condition. The controller will then apply speed-command limits, rate limits, and the minimum-flow constraint. The resulting request will be transmitted only through an interface approved for the selected pump.

The software should use explicit operating states such as initialization, sensor validation, normal control, insufficient-flow protection, and fault handling. This state-based structure makes it easier to demonstrate that a faulty sensor cannot be treated as a normal heating demand and that a logging-computer failure cannot disable critical protection. The final controller update period should be chosen from the measured thermal time constants and sensor response characteristics. A fast electronic loop is not necessarily beneficial when the physical temperature response is much slower, particularly if measurement noise would produce repeated speed changes.


## Interface with Other Subsystems

Provide detailed information about the inputs, outputs, and data transferred to other subsystems. Ensure specificity and thoroughness, clarifying the method of communication and the nature of the data transmitted.



The interface definitions are based on the six-subsystem architecture established in the conceptual design. The Sensor and Data Acquisition subsystem is responsible for obtaining and conditioning field measurements, while this subsystem is responsible for interpreting the resulting values and making the control decision. The Pump and Motor Control subsystem is responsible for converting the requested speed into an actual pump operating condition. The Water Heating and Hydraulic subsystem represents the physical equipment whose response closes the feedback loop. Monitoring and Performance Evaluation evaluates performance, and Data Logging and Simulation preserves the underlying records and provides model-based investigation. These boundaries prevent overlapping responsibility for direct mains-power switching, boiler safety functions, and experimental data analysis.

The controller's inputs originate from the sensor acquisition and heating-system interfaces. The water flow input reports circulation flow in gallons per minute after scaling, while the inlet and outlet temperature inputs provide water temperatures in a consistent unit. The boiler firing-rate input is expected to be an analog 0–10 V signal, interpreted as a percentage after the manufacturer confirms its meaning. Each interface must be documented with the physical connector, electrical range, signal reference, expected update rate, and fault indication. These details are necessary because a diagram showing only a sensor name and an arrow is not sufficient for reliable construction.

The output to the Pump and Motor Control subsystem is a speed request whose electrical form remains to be selected. The final drive may accept an analog 0–10 V signal, PWM, or a digital command. These methods are not interchangeable, and the choice determines the output hardware and the response to loss of controller power. The controller must also transmit data to the logging computer through a supported serial or USB interface. A proposed record contains elapsed time, flow, inlet temperature, outlet temperature, firing-rate percentage, requested speed, operating state, and fault code. Any independently measured electrical power should be clearly identified as a separate instrument reading rather than a controller measurement unless an appropriate input exists.

Integrate a buildable electrical schematic directly into the document. If the diagram is unreadable or improperly scaled, the supervisor will deny approval. Divide the diagram into sections if the text and components seem too small.

The schematic should be relevant to the design and provide ample details necessary for constructing the model. It must be comprehensive so that someone, with no prior knowledge of the design, can easily understand it. Each related component's value and measurement should be clearly mentioned.

The reference electrical architecture is shown in the accompanying diagram, subsystem2_reference_circuit.svg. It identifies the proposed controller, sensor interfaces, firing-rate input conditioning, pump command module, power conversion, and data connection. This diagram is intentionally labeled as a reference architecture rather than a final buildable schematic because the exact field sensor output and pump command requirements are not yet confirmed. It is appropriate for explaining how the subsystem will be organized, but it must be replaced or supplemented with a component-level schematic before construction approval.

A preliminary 0–10 V measurement example uses a divider with R1 = 22 kΩ from the field signal to the ADC node and R2 = 10 kΩ from that node to the signal reference. For an ideal 10 V input, the divider produces V_ADC = 10 × 10/(22 + 10) = 3.125 V, which is below a nominal 3.3 V ADC full-scale value. This example does not address all fault cases: a higher-than-expected input, ground-potential differences, surge events, or resistor failure could still damage the controller. A finalized design requires a reviewed protection strategy, resistor tolerances and power ratings, filtering, and confirmation that the signal source permits the divider's input loading. The field reference must not be tied to the microcontroller ground without evaluating the equipment grounding requirements.

For temperature acquisition, two thermocouple-interface modules based on a device such as the MAX31856 are possible reference components. Each sensor would connect to the appropriate thermocouple input terminals, while the digital interface would connect to a supported microcontroller SPI peripheral with separate chip-select signals. The selected thermocouple type, module supply voltage, logic compatibility, filtering, and connector polarity must be confirmed. The flow input requires its own final circuit after the Keyence model and output mode are known. A 24 V industrial sensor output must not be connected directly to a 3.3 V logic pin. The pump-speed output must likewise use a compatible, appropriately isolated commercial interface or a fully engineered circuit that meets the drive's command specification. No mains-voltage pump wiring is proposed on the microcontroller board.

## Flowchart

For sections including a software component, produce a chart that demonstrates the decision-making process of the microcontroller. It should provide an overview of the device's function without exhaustive detail.


The controller flowchart, subsystem2_flowchart.svg, describes the proposed decision making sequence. After initialization, the controller acquires flow, firing-rate, and temperature measurements and checks their validity. An invalid required input causes the program to enter an approved fault state and record the reason. Valid measurements are used to calculate an initial pump-speed request and a temperature-feedback correction. The controller then applies command limits and checks measured flow against the required minimum before issuing a command.

The minimum-flow decision is a separate branch because it represents an operating constraint rather than an ordinary temperature correction. If flow is insufficient, the program must execute a protective response that has been approved for the actual equipment. The controller records the resulting state and continues monitoring for recovery conditions. The logging computer does not make this safety decision. The diagram uses decision diamonds for input validity and flow protection so that the operating sequence is clear without reproducing every software statement.

## Analysis

Deliver a full and relevant analysis of the design demonstrating that it should meet the constraints and accomplish the intended function. This analysis should be comprehensive and well articulated for persuasiveness.



The conceptual design requires the controller to process available flow, temperature, and boiler measurements; calculate an operating-point-dependent speed request; keep commands within established limits; provide relevant operating data; and respond to missing or implausible measurements. These requirements must be tested individually before integrated testing. The intended Regent Commercial Instantaneous Water Heater remains the design target, but the project proposal explicitly permits compatible Lochinvar laboratory equipment or simulation if access is limited. Therefore, an acceptance result obtained on a different test rig must identify the equipment and cannot automatically be generalized to the Regent without further verification.

The ±4°F temperature target, manufacturer minimum flow, time-to-setpoint, and electrical energy use are coupled constraints. For example, a lower pump command may reduce motor electrical power but may also alter circulation flow and heat transfer. A valid controller decision must be evaluated against all relevant constraints rather than against pump speed alone. The controller must also retain the boiler's own protections; the external algorithm is not a substitute for equipment safety interlocks.

The engineering rationale for the proposed controller is that pump electrical consumption may be reduced when circulation speed can be lowered without violating heating or hydraulic requirements. For a centrifugal pump operating under conditions where affinity-law approximations are appropriate, power is sometimes represented as varying approximately with the cube of rotational speed. The illustrative relationship is P₂/P₁ ≈ (N₂/N₁)³. At 80% of the reference speed, the idealized power ratio is 0.8³ = 0.512, corresponding to a theoretical reduction of 48.8%. This value is not a prediction for the Lochinvar system. Actual electrical power depends on the pump curve, motor efficiency, drive losses, system resistance, and operating point, so final conclusions must be based on measured electrical power.

Temperature performance introduces a competing objective. Lowering flow may increase the temperature rise across a heat exchanger under some conditions, but the boiler's internal control response and system dynamics may change the overall result. The controller therefore cannot rely on a fixed assumption that increasing or decreasing speed always moves the outlet temperature in a particular direction. The relationship should be characterized experimentally and represented in the simulation before feedback gains or lookup-table corrections are finalized. A rate limit and an appropriately chosen control interval may reduce unnecessary command fluctuations, while the minimum-flow constraint prevents the efficiency objective from overriding equipment protection.

The firing-rate measurement also requires an electrical accuracy analysis. With the example 22 kΩ/10 kΩ divider, the nominal scaling factor is 0.3125, and the original signal can be reconstructed as V_firing = 3.2 × V_ADC. The accuracy of that reconstruction depends on resistor tolerance, ADC calibration, reference voltage, source impedance, and noise. Thermocouple accuracy must similarly account for sensor installation, interface error, and cold-junction compensation. These measurement uncertainties matter because the controller is expected to regulate a relatively narrow temperature band. A final design analysis should calculate the combined temperature measurement uncertainty and confirm that the selected hardware is suitable for evaluating the ±4°F target.

Verification will proceed from low-risk interface tests to integrated operation. The firing-rate input can be evaluated using a controlled low-voltage source, and the thermocouple interfaces can be checked against a reference temperature measurement. The flow input should be validated against the selected sensor's calibration and a suitable reference. Pump-command outputs should initially be tested with a simulated or disconnected load that represents the intended command interface, not with unreviewed wiring to an operating boiler. Fault tests should include invalid sensor values, missing records, and controller restart. Integrated tests will then compare baseline and variable-speed operation under comparable thermal conditions, recording minimum flow, outlet-temperature deviation, time-to-setpoint, and measured pump electrical energy.

The principal limitations are the unconfirmed boiler model, flow threshold, pump command method, sensor electrical outputs, and control coefficients. These are not minor documentation omissions; they determine whether the final circuit and controller behavior are safe and technically correct. The reference implementation establishes a defensible architecture, but completion requires component selection, sponsor review, a buildable schematic, and experimental validation.
