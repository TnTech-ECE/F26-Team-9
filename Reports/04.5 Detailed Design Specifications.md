## General Requirements for the Document

The objectives of this subsystem are to:

1. Develop a Dymola model representing the relevant hydraulic and thermal behavior of the selected physical bench.
2. Use simulation results to identify preliminary controller settings and experimental operating conditions.
3. Receive and store measurements from the STM32 microcontroller through a documented communication interface.
4. Incorporate measured pump and drive real electrical input power into the experimental dataset.
5. Organize data using timestamps, test identifiers, units, and operating-condition labels.
6. Support repeated baseline and controlled trials under comparable conditions.
7. Calculate electrical power savings and relevant temperature and flow performance measures.
8. Compare simulated and measured responses to evaluate model accuracy.
9. Document uncertainty, missing measurements, assumptions, and unsuccessful trials.
10. Produce graphs, tables, and supporting evidence for the final technical report.


## Function of the Subsystem

The Data Logging and Simulation Subsystem is responsible for collecting, storing, organizing, and analyzing the experimental measurements generated by the Variable-Speed Circulation Pump Controller. This subsystem also supports the development and validation of the control strategy through computer simulation using Dymola.

The overall project investigates whether adjusting circulation pump speed according to water flow, heating demand, and temperature feedback can reduce electrical power consumption while maintaining acceptable thermal and hydraulic performance. The intended future application is the Lochinvar Regent Commercial Tankless Water Heater. However, the initial design will be evaluated using a required physical bench-testing system consisting of a variable speed circulation pump, water loop, heat source, flow sensor, temperature sensors, and the STM32 based controller. The Data Logging and Simulation Subsystem serves two primary functions.

The first function is simulation. Dymola will represent the expected behavior of the physical circulation loop, including the relationships between pump operation, water flow, heat transfer, and temperature response. Simulation results will be used to select preliminary controller settings and operating conditions for physical testing. This reduces the amount of trial-and-error required during initial controller development and allows potential performance limitations to be investigated before applying changes to the bench.

The second function is experimental data logging and analysis. Measurements from the physical bench will be recorded in a structured format so that baseline and variable-speed operation can be compared. The system will preserve flow measurements, inlet and outlet temperatures, representative heating demand, pump commands, electrical input power, operating states, and fault information.

The physical bench test will provide the primary experimental evidence for evaluating the controller. Simulation results will not replace physical testing. Instead, the simulation will be compared against the measured response of the bench to determine how accurately the model represents actual system behavior.

This subsystem will not be responsible for directly controlling the circulation pump during normal operation. The Microcontroller and Control Logic Subsystem will execute the real time control algorithm. The Data Logging and Simulation Subsystem will support that algorithm through model development, preliminary parameter selection, measurement recording, and performance analysis.

#### Communication Compatibility

The STM32 Nucleo-L476RG and the computer based logging application must use compatible communication settings. The preliminary design uses USB serial communication through the development board's supported serial interface. The final configuration must establish the selected serial port, baud rate, message format, data types, and error-handling procedure.

#### Measurement Timing

The preliminary data recording interval is no greater than one second for the primary controller measurements. The selected communication and storage implementation must support this requirement without losing measurements during normal operation. The exact sampling rates of individual sensors may differ from the logging interval. The system will distinguish sensor acquisition timing from the rate at which records are stored.

#### Data Storage

The subsystem must retain enough information to reproduce the experimental analysis. Raw data will be preserved separately from processed results. CSV files will contain column headings, consistent units, and identifiers for the test conditions. Model parameters and software versions will also be documented.


#### Electrical Power Measurement

The project requires measurement of real electrical input power rather than an estimate based only on RMS voltage and current. The proposed PZEM-004T V3.0 remains a candidate instrument. Its suitability, accuracy, safety requirements, electrical isolation, and supported communication method must be verified before use. If it is unsuitable, an approved alternative instrument will be selected.


#### Simulation Accuracy

Dymola predictions depend on the quality of the pump curves, hydraulic parameters, thermal properties, and operating assumptions used in the model. The simulation must not be presented as experimentally validated until its predictions have been compared with measured bench data.

#### Budget and Equipment Availability

The design should use the available STM32 development board, computer resources, and Dymola software where possible. Additional equipment must remain within the approved capstone budget.

### Standards, Ethics, and Socioeconomic Constraints

Data integrity is an essential engineering responsibility because the results may influence future decisions about energy efficiency and equipment operation. The subsystem shall preserve original measurements, document calibration information, report unsuccessful trials, and distinguish theoretical predictions from measured results. These practices support the professional responsibility to present technical information accurately and without misleading claims. Any sponsor provided models, datasets, or proprietary technical information will be handled according to applicable Lochinvar and Tennessee Tech requirements.

The logging computer and low voltage communication connections shall not bypass electrical isolation or protective arrangements required for the bench. Applicable laboratory electrical safety procedures and manufacturer instructions will govern the connection of measurement equipment. The subsystem will also consider economic feasibility by using available software and development equipment where practical, without sacrificing measurement quality or experimental traceability.


## Overview of Proposed Solution

The proposed subsystem consists of four functional elements: a computer based data logger, organized data storage, a Dymola simulation model, and a post processing and analysis application.

### 1. Computer Based Data Logger

A Python application will be developed to receive serial messages from the STM32 controller. The program will open the selected serial port, read incoming messages, check the message structure, identify invalid records, and save valid measurements in CSV format. Python is selected because it supports serial communication, numerical calculations, data analysis, and graph generation using commonly available libraries.

* The preliminary implementation will use:
* Python for application development.
* PySerial for serial communication.
* Pandas for organizing and processing experimental data.
* NumPy for numerical calculations.
* Matplotlib for graph generation.
* The Python standard library for file handling, timestamps, and basic validation.

These libraries are proposed implementation tools, not evidence that the final application has already been completed.

### 2. Experimental Data Storage

CSV files will store the measurements collected during each physical test. Each test will have a unique identifier, operating condition label, and baseline or controlled designation. A separate metadata record will identify the selected equipment, sensor configuration, test conditions, calibration information, software version, and any unusual events observed during testing. The raw measurement files will remain unchanged after collection. Processed data and calculated results will be saved separately.

### 3. Dymola Simulation

Dymola will represent the relevant behavior of the physical water circulation loop. The model will include the selected pump's operating characteristics, hydraulic resistance, water properties, heat-source behavior, and temperature response to the extent supported by verified equipment information. The simulation will be used before physical testing to investigate how changes in pump operation affect flow and temperature behavior. Preliminary controller settings and test conditions will be selected from these results and evaluated using the physical bench.

### 4. Performance Analysis

After the physical bench tests are completed, the recorded measurements will be processed to calculate electrical-power savings, temperature stability, flow behavior, and response time. The same operating conditions will be applied to the Dymola model for comparison. The final analysis will identify differences between predicted and measured performance and determine whether the controller satisfies the established project requirements.

### Communication Error Handling

The logger will check the number of received fields and whether numerical values can be converted into their required data types. Messages that are incomplete, malformed, or inconsistent with the selected format will be flagged. The program will record communication interruptions and prevent missing measurements from being silently replaced with invented values. If communication stops, the logger will preserve the records already collected and notify the operator. The logging application will not independently override the STM32's pump-control decisions.

### Build Procedure

The Data Logging and Simulation Subsystem will use the STM32 Nucleo-L476RG to transmit experimental measurements to a computer through USB serial communication while simultaneously maintaining local backup storage on a microSD card. The following procedure describes the proposed hardware assembly, software configuration, and verification process.

**Step 1: Prepare the STM32 and microSD hardware.**

Obtain the STM32 Nucleo-L476RG development board, a 3.3 V compatible SPI microSD card module, a microSD card, appropriate connection wires, and a USB communication cable. Confirm that the selected microSD module supports the intended 3.3 V supply and STM32 logic levels. Format the memory card using a supported FAT file system before testing.

**Step 2: Connect the microSD module to the STM32.**

With power disconnected, connect the microSD module using the proposed SPI1 configuration.

| STM32 Pin | Arduino Label | microSD Module Pin | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| PA5 | D13 | SCK | SPI clock |
| PA6 | D12 | MISO | Data transmitted from microSD to STM32 |
| PA7 | D11 | MOSI | Data transmitted from STM32 to microSD |
| PB6 | D10 | CS | Chip select |
| 3V3 | Power header | VCC | 3.3 V supply |
| GND | Ground header | GND | Common ground |

These assignments must match the STM32CubeMX configuration and the final electrical schematic. If the MAX31865 temperature sensor modules share SPI1, each device must use an independent chip select signal, and only one device may be selected during a transaction.

**Step 3: Initialize the STM32 logging interfaces.**

Configure the STM32 firmware to initialize the required GPIO pins, SPI1 peripheral, and UART interface. The preliminary UART settings are 115200 baud, eight data bits, no parity, and one stop bit. The firmware will initialize the microSD interface and attempt to mount the card's file system using a compatible SPI-based storage driver.

**Step 4: Check microSD availability.**

After initialization, the firmware will determine whether the microSD card is detected, the file system is accessible, and a logging file can be opened. If initialization fails, the controller will record a storage fault and continue through the defined fault handling procedure. A microSD failure must not be treated as a successful backup operation.

**Step 5: Configure the Python data logger.**

Install Python and the required libraries on the logging computer. Configure the application to open the STM32's USB serial port, receive newline terminated measurement records, validate incoming values, and save accepted records to primary CSV files.

**Step 6: Acquire experimental measurements.**

During physical bench testing, the STM32 will collect the available flow rate, inlet temperature, outlet temperature, heating demand, pump command, controller state, and fault information. Each logging record will include a timestamp or sequence identifier that allows measurements to be associated with the correct experimental trial.

**Step 7: Check USB communication and store primary records.**

The firmware will transmit each measurement record through the USB serial communication pathway. When the connection is available, the Python application will validate the record and save the accepted measurements to the primary CSV file. Incomplete or invalid records will be flagged for review.

If USB communication is interrupted, the system will record the communication fault and proceed with microSD backup logging, provided that the controller and storage hardware remain operational.

**Step 8: Write backup measurements to the microSD card.**

When the microSD card is available, the STM32 will write the corresponding timestamped measurement record to the backup CSV file. The preliminary design requires a logging interval of no greater than one second. The firmware will check the result of each storage operation and flag unsuccessful writes.

The backup file will preserve the information necessary to associate measurements with the correct test identifier and operating mode. The selected buffering and file synchronization strategy will be verified to reduce the risk of losing records during an interruption.

**Step 9: Determine whether testing should continue.**

After completing the primary and backup logging operations, the system will evaluate whether the experimental test is still active.

If testing continues, the STM32 will return to the measurement acquisition step and repeat the logging process. If testing is complete, the system will proceed to the file-closing procedure.

**Step 10: Safely close the logging files.**

At the end of testing, the firmware will synchronize any pending microSD data and close the backup file. The Python application will flush and close the primary CSV file. The operator will verify that logging has stopped before disconnecting the system or removing the microSD card.

**Step 11: Verify the primary and backup datasets.**

Open the primary computer CSV file and the microSD backup file. Compare timestamps, test identifiers, operating modes, record counts, and available measurements. Document missing records, communication interruptions, and storage faults. Differences between the two datasets will be investigated rather than silently corrected.

**Step 12: Import electrical power measurements.**

Import the real electrical input power measurements from the approved power monitoring instrument. Associate the measurements with the corresponding test identifiers and observation periods. Confirm that power data are aligned with the recorded flow, temperature, and pump command measurements.

**Step 13: Compare experimental results with Dymola predictions.**

Import the physical bench measurements and the corresponding Dymola simulation results into the analysis application. Compare results under equivalent operating conditions and calculate the selected model error measures.

**Step 14: Calculate performance metrics and generate results.**

Use the recorded data to calculate electrical power savings, energy consumption, outlet temperature deviation, flow behavior, and applicable statistical measures. Generate graphs and summary tables for the technical report. Preserve the original raw data separately from processed results.

**Step 15: Perform final subsystem verification.**

Complete integration tests demonstrating successful USB logging, microSD initialization, CSV writing, USB interruption recovery through backup logging, storage fault detection, safe file closure, and comparison of primary and backup datasets. Record the test results and any remaining implementation limitations before conducting official bench experiments.

## Analysis

The Data Logging and Simulation Subsystem must provide reliable information that can be used to evaluate whether the variable-speed controller satisfies the project's performance requirements.

The following analysis explains the mathematical relationships, software decisions, and experimental procedures supporting the proposed design.

| Parameter | Value | Description |
|---|---:|---|
| Logging interval | 1 second | Preliminary maximum interval |
| Logging frequency | 1 record/s | Calculated from logging interval |
| Illustrative test duration | 600 seconds | 10-minute example |
| Records per illustrative test | 600 | Based on one record per second |
| Illustrative record size | 200 bytes | Assumed CSV record size |
| Storage per test | 120,000 bytes | Approximately 120 kB |
| Number of paired comparisons | 15 | Three conditions with five pairs each |
| Number of individual runs | 30 | Baseline and controlled runs |
| Illustrative raw storage total | 3,600,000 bytes | Approximately 3.6 MB |


### 1. Data Logging Interval and Storage Requirements

The preliminary logging requirement is one measurement record per second or faster.

For a logging interval of:

$$
\Delta t=1\text{ s}
$$

the corresponding record rate is:

$$
f_s=\frac{1}{\Delta t}
$$

$$
f_s=1\text{ record/s}
$$

For a test lasting 10 minutes:

$$
t=10(60)=600\text{ s}
$$

The expected number of records is approximately:

$$
N=\frac{600}{1}=600
$$

The actual number will depend on whether both the initial and final timestamps are included.

If each stored record occupies an illustrative 200 bytes, the approximate data volume for one 10-minute test is:

$$
D=N(200)
$$

$$
D=600(200)=120000\text{ bytes}
$$

This is approximately 120 kB using decimal units.

For 30 individual runs, corresponding to 15 baseline/controlled pairs:

$$
D_{\text{total}}=30(120000)
$$

$$
D_{\text{total}}=3600000\text{ bytes}
$$

Therefore, approximately 3.6 MB of raw CSV storage would be required under these illustrative assumptions, excluding metadata, additional channels, and processed files. The calculation indicates that the expected data volume is manageable using ordinary computer storage. The 10-minute test duration and 200-byte record size are examples used for preliminary capacity planning, not finalized experimental requirements.

### 2. Serial Communication Capacity

The preliminary UART configuration uses a baud rate of 115200 bits/s.

Assuming an 8-N-1 configuration, each transmitted byte requires approximately 10 serial bits.

The approximate maximum byte rate before protocol overhead and processing limitations is:

$$
R_{\text{byte}}=\frac{115200}{10}
$$

$$
R_{\text{byte}}=11520\text{ bytes/s}
$$

If each transmitted measurement message contains an illustrative 150 bytes and is sent once per second:

$$
R_{\text{required}}=150\text{ bytes/s}
$$

The approximate fraction of serial capacity used is:

$$
U=\frac{150}{11520}\times100
$$

$$
U\approx1.30%
$$

This suggests that the proposed serial link has sufficient nominal throughput for the planned one second logging interval. Actual performance will depend on firmware timing, message size, USB serial implementation, computer processing, and error handling. Communication performance will therefore be verified experimentally.


### 3. Electrical Power Savings

The primary project performance objective is to determine whether the variable-speed controller can reduce measured pump-and-drive real electrical input power by at least 10% under qualifying operating conditions.

Electrical savings will be calculated using:

$$
S=\frac{P_{\text{baseline}}-P_{\text{controlled}}}{P_{\text{baseline}}}\times100%
$$

where:

* $P_{\text{baseline}}$ is measured baseline real electrical input power.

* $P_{\text{controlled}}$ is measured controlled real electrical input power.

* $S$ is percentage power savings.

For an illustrative example, assume:

$$
P_{\text{baseline}}=60\text{ W}
$$

$$
P_{\text{controlled}}=51\text{ W}
$$

Then:

$$
S=\frac{60-51}{60}\times100
$$

$$
S=15%
$$

This example demonstrates the calculation method and does not represent measured project results. The actual calculation will use average real power over equivalent observation windows for matched baseline and controlled trials. Power savings will not be considered a successful outcome if the controlled operating condition violates the approved flow, temperature, or safety requirements.

### 4. Electrical Energy Consumption

Electrical power represents the rate of energy consumption. The energy consumed over an operating period can be calculated by integrating power with respect to time:

$$
E=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}P(t)\,dt
$$

For approximately constant power:

$$
E=P\Delta t
$$

For example, a pump operating at an illustrative 60 W for 10 minutes consumes:

$$
E=60\left(\frac{10}{60}\right)
$$

$$
E=10\text{ Wh}
$$

At an illustrative 51 W over the same duration:

$$
E=51\left(\frac{10}{60}\right)
$$

$$
E=8.5\text{ Wh}
$$

The difference is:

$$
\Delta E=10-8.5=1.5\text{ Wh}
$$

This example demonstrates how measured power can be converted into energy consumption. It does not establish actual savings for the selected bench pump.

### 5. Heat Transfer Model

The Dymola simulation will represent the relationship between water flow rate, temperature change, and thermal energy transfer.

The heat-transfer rate to the water is:

$$
\dot{Q}h=\dot{m}c_p(T{\text{out}}-T_{\text{in}})
$$

where:

* $\dot{Q}_h$ is the heat-transfer rate.
* $\dot{m}$ is the mass flow rate.
* $c_p$ is the specific heat capacity of water.
* $T_{\text{out}}$ is outlet-water temperature.
* $T_{\text{in}}$ is inlet-water temperature.

Mass flow rate is related to volumetric flow rate by:

$$
\dot{m}=\rho\dot{V}
$$

For preliminary calculations using liquid water, an approximate relationship is:

$$
\dot{Q}_h\approx500(\text{GPM})(\Delta T)
$$

where $\dot{Q}_h$ is in Btu/h and $\Delta T$ is in °F.

These equations will help establish the expected temperature rise across the physical heat source under different flow conditions. The simulation will use the selected equipment's actual characteristics when they become available rather than relying entirely on illustrative values.


### 6. Reservoir Thermal Behavior

The physical bench may include a recirculating reservoir. Its temperature response must be considered because continuous heating can cause the entire water volume to increase in temperature.

A simplified reservoir energy balance is:

$$
m_wc_p\frac{dT}{dt}=P_{\text{heat,water}}-\dot{Q}_{\text{loss}}
$$

where:

* $m_w$ is the mass of water in the reservoir.
* $c_p$ is the specific heat capacity of water.
* $T$ is reservoir temperature.
* $P_{\text{heat,water}}$ is thermal power delivered to the water.
* $\dot{Q}_{\text{loss}}$ is heat loss to the surroundings.

This relationship is important because pump-speed control alone may not maintain a constant outlet temperature in a continuously heated closed loop. The Dymola model will account for the selected thermal arrangement to the extent supported by the available data. The physical test plan must establish repeatable thermal conditions through an appropriate heater operating procedure, heat-removal method, or defined transient test.

### 7. Pump Performance Modeling

The simulation will use manufacturer pump characteristics and the pump affinity laws as preliminary tools for predicting the effect of speed changes.

The theoretical relationships are:

$$
\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}=\frac{N_2}{N_1}
$$

$$
\frac{H_2}{H_1}=\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^2
$$

$$
\frac{P_2}{P_1}=\left(\frac{N_2}{N_1}\right)^3
$$

where $Q$ is flow rate, $H$ is pump head, $P$ is mechanical pump power, and $N$ is rotational speed.

For an illustrative speed reduction to 80%:

$$
\frac{P_2}{P_1}=(0.80)^3=0.512
$$

This corresponds to a theoretical mechanical power reduction of:

$$
(1-0.512)\times100=48.8%
$$

This value is not the predicted measured electrical savings of the project. Actual electrical consumption depends on motor efficiency, drive losses, hydraulic resistance, and the pump's operating characteristics. The simulation will therefore use verified pump curves when available, and electrical savings will be established through measured real input power.

### 8. Simulation Validation

Simulation results will be compared with physical bench measurements under equivalent conditions.

For a measured variable $x$, the absolute prediction error is:

$$
e_x=|x_{\text{simulated}}-x_{\text{measured}}|
$$

Where the measured value is nonzero and a percentage comparison is meaningful:

$$
e_{\mathrm{percent}} = \frac{\left|x_{\mathrm{simulated}} - x_{\mathrm{measured}}\right|}{\left|x_{\mathrm{measured}}\right|} \times 100
$$

For a series of $n$ measurements, root mean square error may be calculated as:

$$
RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{\text{simulated},i}-x_{\text{measured},i})^2}
$$

The comparison will include selected flow-rate and temperature measurements, as well as relevant transient response characteristics. Simulation and measured data must be aligned using comparable operating conditions and time references before error calculations are performed. If the model does not match the physical results, the team will examine the pump parameters, heat-transfer assumptions, hydraulic resistance, initial conditions, and measurement uncertainty. Model parameters adjusted using one set of experimental data should be evaluated against separate validation data where feasible, reducing the risk of presenting a fitted model as independently validated.


### 9. Statistical Analysis of Repeated Trials

The preliminary experimental plan includes three demand conditions: low, medium, and high. Five paired baseline and controlled trials will be performed at each condition.

The total number of paired comparisons is:

$$
N_{\text{pairs}}=3(5)=15
$$

Each pair will compare baseline and controlled operation under equivalent test conditions.

The mean percentage savings for a set of paired trials is:

$$
\bar{S}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}S_i
$$

The sample standard deviation is:

$$
s=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(S_i-\bar{S})^2}{n-1}}
$$

When the assumptions are appropriate, a two-sided 95% confidence interval for the mean savings is:

$$
CI_{\mathrm{95percent}} = \bar{S} \pm t_{0.975,n-1}\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

The team will report the sample size, mean savings, variability, and confidence interval for each operating condition when supported by the data. The analysis will avoid treating multiple time samples from a single trial as independent experimental repetitions. The trial or matched pair will be the primary unit for evaluating repeatability.

### 10. Temperature Stability and Response Analysis

The project includes a preliminary outlet-temperature stability target of ±4°F.

The temperature error is:

$$
e_T(t)=T_{\text{setpoint}}-T_{\text{out}}(t)
$$

The absolute temperature deviation is:

$$
|e_T(t)|=|T_{\text{setpoint}}-T_{\text{out}}(t)|
$$

For the defined steady-state observation period, the maximum absolute deviation will be calculated as:

$$
e_{T,\max}=\max_i|e_T(t_i)|
$$

The preliminary requirement is:

$$
e_{T,\max}\leq4^\circ\text{F}
$$

over the approved 60-second steady-state observation window.

The analysis will also calculate the standard deviation of outlet temperature during the selected window. Following a defined demand change, the recovery time will be determined from the measurement record. The preliminary objective is to return to within ±4°F of the setpoint within 120 seconds and remain within the band for at least 30 seconds. The subsystem will report the measured response rather than assuming that the controller meets these targets.


### 11. Data Integrity and Fault Handling

The accuracy of the final performance analysis depends on the reliability of the recorded data. The logging application will perform basic validation before storing measurements. These checks will include field count, data type, timestamp progression, and the presence of required information. Out of range measurements will be flagged for review rather than automatically deleted. The logger will preserve information about missing data, communication interruptions, and unsuccessful trials.

The final analysis will distinguish between:

* Raw measurements obtained directly from the experimental system.
* Processed measurements calculated from raw data.
* Simulated values generated by Dymola.
* Preliminary engineering targets.
* Manufacturer published equipment specifications.
* Assumptions used in calculations or model development.
* This distinction will help prevent unsupported conclusions and support reproducibility.

4. Pump-command and controller state histories.

## Conclusion

The Data Logging and Simulation Subsystem will provide the measurement records, simulation predictions, and engineering analysis required to evaluate the proposed Variable Speed Circulation Pump Controller. The selected approach combines Dymola modeling with a computer-based Python logging and analysis application. Dymola will support preliminary controller development and selection of physical test conditions, while the STM32 and approved measurement equipment will provide the experimental data required for validation. The subsystem will organize the collected measurements into structured CSV files, calculate electrical-power savings, evaluate temperature and flow behavior, and compare the physical bench response with simulation predictions.

By preserving raw measurements, documenting assumptions, and evaluating repeatability and uncertainty, the subsystem will support technically defensible conclusions about the proposed controller. The remaining implementation decisions include confirming the exact power monitoring interface, final simulation parameters, equipment specific operating limits, and communication configuration. These details will be finalized and documented before experimental verification. The completed design will allow the team to evaluate the controller using measured results rather than relying only on theoretical calculations or simulation predictions.
